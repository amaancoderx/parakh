# Pipeline-level lessons from non-English ASR (Arabic, African, SE Asian, Indic, code-switched, European minority) and what transfers to Parakh

Scope note: 2022–2026 focus. Many sources are 2026 arXiv preprints (IDs 26xx.xxxxx) or vendor pages; these are flagged. "(snippet)" means the claim came from a search-result summary of the paper and the full text was not fetched; treat as lower confidence. Items already covered by earlier research (RECOVER, DeRAGEC, Apple contacts, IndicContextEval, Voice of India, misheard, NeMo ITN) are deliberately not repeated.

## 1. Arabic: dialect handling, orthographic normalisation in scoring, Arabic–English/French code-switching, products, and what multi-system / LLM post-correction added

### Takeaway
Arabic practice converges on three harness-level moves: (a) normalise orthography (alef/hamza, ta marbuta, alef maqsura/ya, diacritics, tatweel) *before* scoring or voting, because WER otherwise punishes spelling variants and transliteration choices (WER inflated system gaps about 3× against a semantic metric in one 2026 code-switching benchmark); (b) dialect-ID routing helps only when the router is accurate (predicted routing gave back part of the oracle gain, and in one case made things worse); (c) test-time tricks that need no fine-tuning, such as prompting a promptable decoder with another system's first-pass hypothesis or picking the n-best candidate closest to other systems' outputs, gave 9–22% relative WER reductions. ROVER and system combination gave the last fraction of a point in NADI 2026.

### Cited Findings
**Dialect ID → routing**
- VoxArabica runs a dialect-ID model first (fine-tuned HuBERT, 18 labels: country-level dialects plus MSA). The predicted label picks which dialect-specific Whisper ASR model runs, [VoxArabica, arXiv 2310.11069](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.11069)
- Itgan at NADI 2026 (Whisper + LoRA). On country-level ASR, per-dialect specialist adapters continued from a pooled adapter gave the largest gain. A linear-probe router on frozen encoder features "recovers 44% of what oracle routing gives". Headline WERs: 57.1% country-average (1.1), 46.7% mixed-dialect (1.2, after adding a "decorrelated system-combination member"), 14.49% WER / 5.38% CER on Tunisian code-switched ASR (1.3, 2nd place). On 1.3, weight-space averaging of independent runs gave most of the last 0.60 WER points and ROVER voting gave the rest, [Itgan NADI 2026, arXiv 2610.09934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.09934). (snippet) The full paper reportedly puts gold routing at 1.9 WER points (3.4% relative), [search summary of 2610.09934](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.09934)
- (snippet) GigaSpeech 3: predicted LID instead of gold dialect labels raises macro-average WER from 23.62 to 25.51 across five dialects. The authors call reliable automatic dialect ID "unresolved", [GigaSpeech 3, arXiv 2611.02476](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.02476)
- (snippet) A patent family reports that training separate models on dialect subsets lowered WER compared with one model on all data, [USPTO 9966064](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9966064)
- A text-only dialect router model card warns that code-switched text (Arabic + French in Darija, Arabic + English) confuses the classifier, [dialect-router-v0.1, Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/yrrhall/dialect-router-v0.1)

**Orthographic normalisation in scoring**
- (snippet) The Almieyar 2026 multi-dialect benchmark scores after one shared normalisation: it removes diacritics and tatweel, unifies alef/hamza forms, normalises ta marbuta and alef maqsura, and collapses punctuation. It says CER is more informative than WER for Arabic because of clitics and morphology, [Almieyar, arXiv 2609.35564](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.35564)
- Ali, Khalifa & Habash (Interspeech 2019, pre-2022 but still the reference work): dialectal Arabic has no fixed orthography. WER variants with spelling normalisation and morphological abstraction correlated better with human judgements, [ISCA archive](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2019/ali19_interspeech.html)
- Letter normalisation reduces spelling inconsistency but adds ambiguity and hurts readability, so it belongs in scoring and matching, not in the displayed text, [arXiv 2507.13977](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.13977v1)
- (snippet) SN-WER reports Arabic WER reductions of about 4.9–6.9% under script-normalised scoring, [SN-WER, arXiv 2606.02548](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.02548)
- On dialectal Arabic–English speech, the metric that best matched human judgement was "transliteration followed by text normalization", with clear minimal-editing guidelines for references (4-way inter-annotator agreement), [Benchmarking Evaluation Metrics for CS ASR, arXiv 2211.16319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16319)

**Arabic–English code-switching benchmarks and products**
- Perle AI (May 2026 preprint) tested 5 commercial ASR APIs on Egyptian Arabic–English, Saudi (Najdi/Hijazi)–English, Persian–English and German–English, 300 samples per pair. ElevenLabs Scribe v2 was best (13.2% WER overall, 13.1% Egyptian Arabic; BERTScore 0.936). WER and BERTScore ranked systems identically for the Arabic and Persian pairs (τ = 1.0), but "WER inflates the size of quality gaps by about 3×" because it penalises semantically correct transliteration choices. Data: HF `Perle-ai/ASR_Code_Switch`, [arXiv 2605.19069](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19069)
- (snippet) The same paper says production Arabic pipelines usually canonicalise text, for example unifying hamza variants and collapsing ta marbuta into ha, [arXiv 2605.19069 PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.19069)
- PIER on Arabic–English (ArzEn), Whisper-small: fine-tuning moved WER 69.16 → 55.08 while PIER (error on the embedded-language words) got *worse*, 75.91 → 78.10, [PIER, arXiv 2501.09512](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09512v2)
- ArzEn-LLM chains Whisper code-switched Egyptian Arabic ASR into LLM translation. It reports +56% English and +9.3% Arabic translation over the previous state of the art. This is a cascade, not post-correction, [arXiv 2406.18120](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18120v2)
- Speechmatics (vendor page) says it supports MSA plus Egyptian, Gulf, Levantine and Maghrebi Arabic, has a "bilingual" Arabic/English model that handles code-switching "natively", and offers real-time and batch modes. Vendor-reported Arabic WER table: Speechmatics 4.5%, Whisper 6.2%, AssemblyAI 6.5%, Deepgram 7.7%, Amazon 8.3%, Microsoft 10.4%. The test set is not disclosed. The page does not say whether dialects share one model, [Speechmatics Arabic](https://www.speechmatics.com/speech-to-text/arabic)
- Tarteel: a research system using Tarteel's Whisper fine-tune (WER about 5.75%) matches the transcript against the *known* Quran text with Levenshtein fuzzy matching and a moving pointer. Samples under an 85% match go to human annotators, [arXiv 2509.00094](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00094). Tarteel's app describes word-level "missed / wrong / extra word" detection, [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/tarteel-%D8%AA%D8%B1%D8%AA%D9%8A%D9%84-ai-quran/id1391009396). A developer post (not peer-reviewed) says ASR "snaps" vowel-level (harakah) errors back to the canonical form, so alignment to expected text catches word errors but not fine phonetic ones, [HF discussion](https://huggingface.co/Muno459/fastconformer-quran/discussions/2)

**Prompting vs fine-tuning; multi-system and LLM post-correction**
- Talafha, Abu Alhassan, Abdul-Mageed (preprint, 2025), test-time only with no parameter updates. Whisper-large-v3's decoder is prompted with a first-pass hypothesis from a *different* system (SeamlessM4T), or prefixed with retrieved speech–text exemplars. Average MSA WER 15.79 → 12.27 (−22.29% rel); FLEURS accented MSA 9.20 → 7.31 (−20.54%); dialectal 57.48 → 52.22 (−9.15%); MGB-5 79.16 → 68.21. Reversed or shuffled word-order prompts often did as well as or better than in-order prompts, because they "preserve the multiset of prompt words but disrupt sequential coherence". Failure modes: Whisper treats the prompt as text to continue (empty or boilerplate output), hallucinates ("subscribe to the channel"), and degrades when retrieved audio is from a mismatched speaker or domain. Noisy proxy hypotheses can mislead, [arXiv 2511.18774 (HTML)](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18774)
- Same paper, CTC extension ("proxy-guided n-best selection"): pick the n-best candidate with minimum edit distance to an *external* system's hypothesis. Top-1 WER 20.58% → 17.36% at beam 10, against an oracle of 13.49%. The paper says this recovers "70%" of the oracle gain, but its own numbers give about 45%. A multi-proxy weighted distance is also described, [arXiv 2511.18774](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18774)
- Fine-tuning comparisons: small amounts of MSA fine-tuning data give large gains, and fine-tuned small models match larger non-fine-tuned ones, [arXiv 2506.02627](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02627v1). One shared-task system went from about 93.9 to 39.8 WER with dialect-specific Whisper fine-tuning, while a dialect-centric CTC model scored 58.89 zero-shot, [ArabicNLP 2025 shared task](https://preview.aclanthology.org/ingest-emnlp/2025.arabicnlp-sharedtasks.102.pdf)
- (snippet) LLM post-processing reduces errors on dialectal Arabic ASR. Details were not verified, [IU Journal of Applied Sciences, art. 289](https://jesc.iu.edu.sa/Main/Article/289)

### Inferences
- Parakh analogue of alef/hamza/ta-marbuta normalisation: a Devanagari "matching key" that strips nukta (ज़→ज, फ़→फ), unifies chandrabindu and anusvara, removes ZWJ/ZWNJ and halant artefacts, maps Devanagari digits to ASCII, and collapses danda and punctuation. Apply it only for alignment, voting and name matching, never to the displayed text, following the readability trade-off in 2507.13977. Hours-scale.
- The Talafha result is the most directly portable Arabic idea. Parakh already has three recognisers, so it can pass Saaras's first-pass words as the `prompt` to the OpenAI transcriber, if that endpoint accepts a prompt. Pass them as a shuffled bag of words plus candidate names, not as a fluent sentence, to reduce prompt continuation and copying. Test on a held-out set, because noisy prompts can mislead.
- Proxy-guided selection maps to black-box APIs as minimum-Bayes-risk or consensus selection: from the pool of candidates (3 recognisers × modes, e.g. Saaras `transcribe`/`codemix`/`translit`), pick the one with the lowest average normalised edit distance to the others. This is cheap and needs no n-best access.
- Routing lesson: do *not* split Hinglish calls by language or dialect and send each part to a different recogniser. The router is the weak link (44% of the oracle gain recovered at best, and negative in GigaSpeech 3). Run every recogniser on the whole audio and fuse.
- System-combination lesson from NADI: gains come from *decorrelated* members. Parakh should measure pairwise error overlap between Saaras v3, Saaras v4 and gpt-transcribe. If v3 and v4 err on the same tokens, give them less combined voting weight than the counts suggest.

### Gaps
- "Lahajat" as a product or model: no sources found.
- No head-to-head of prompting versus fine-tuning on the same dialectal Arabic set.
- No public evidence of how Speechmatics or other vendors expose Arabic custom vocabulary ("sounds-like") for names.
- No verified numbers for LLM generative error correction on Arabic–English or Arabic–French code-switching specifically. ArzEn-LLM is a translation cascade.

## 2. African languages: Masakhane, Common Voice Swahili, Google USM, Karya-style collection, Lelapa AI (Vulavula), Intron Health (Sahara): names, numbers, code-switching, low-resource conditions

### Takeaway
The best-documented African findings come from Intron Health's papers. Low overall WER hides high error on named entities (clinical entities). LLM post-correction helped *weaker* recognisers but slightly hurt the strongest ones, including an LLM correcting its own transcript, and it introduced insertions and deletions around medications and measurement values. On in-the-wild African–English code-switching, the best zero-shot system averaged about 36% WER, and Africa-targeted training predicted accuracy better than model scale or nominal language coverage. Errors cluster at switch points and on the lower-resource language's tokens.

### Cited Findings
- Clinical English over 93 African accents: several ASR models with low general WER still made many errors on clinical named entities. The paper introduces medical NE recall and an entity-alignment algorithm. Fine-tuning on accented clinical speech improved medical WER by 25–34% relative, [Afonja et al., Interspeech 2024, arXiv 2406.12387](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12387)
- LLM correction across Nigeria (Intron Health), UK (PriMock57) and US (Fareez) medical speech. ASR systems: Gemini 1.5 Pro, Azure STT, Whisper-large-v3, Canary-1B, Amazon Transcribe Medical, Soniox. Correctors: Gemini 1.5 Pro, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, GPT-4o, using a chain-of-thought prompt in 10-line segments at temperature 0–0.1 with JSON output. Nigeria WER: Canary 0.241 → 0.21, Azure 0.274 → 0.239, Soniox 0.175 → 0.163, but Gemini 0.102 → 0.129 (worse). US: Azure 0.142 → 0.085, Gemini 0.062 → 0.098 (worse). Gemini correcting its own transcripts "consistently degraded performance". Medical-concept error on Nigerian data: Azure 48.67% → 46.17% (GPT-4o), Canary 30.16% → 25.52% (Claude), Gemini 10.69% → 12.54% (worse). Correction fixed near-miss spellings ("fexifenadine" → "fexofenadine") but failed on plausible distant substitutions ("metformin" vs "warfarin"). It introduced new insertions on medication terms (+12) and deletions on substance-use terms (+6) and body measurement values (+4), [arXiv 2501.15310](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15310v1)
- AfriSpeech-MultiBench (Intron-affiliated) has a Named Entities domain. Multimodal LLMs are more accent-robust but "struggle with domain-specific named entities", [arXiv 2511.14255](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14255v1)
- AfriSwitch (Intron Health, 2026 preprint): 61.36 h of human-transcribed in-the-wild code-switched speech across 16 African languages and varieties, each mixed with English. Annotations: switch-level English span tags, per-utterance Code-Mixing Index and switch-point counts. Five open and commercial systems were tested zero-shot. The best averaged 35.93% WER, and none went below 24% on any language. Africa-targeted training, not scale or nominal coverage, best predicted performance. The HF card gives different figures (54.41 h, 14 languages), probably a different release, [arXiv 2608.26434](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.26434); [HF dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/intronhealth/AfriSwitch)
- English–Yoruba (2026 preprint): 11 systems (6 ASR, 5 audio LMs) on 2,000 code-switched utterances. The paper adds switch-aware metrics (switch-entry token error rate, windowed switch-point error rates, per-language error rates) and a diacritic-insensitive WER. The best-WER system was an ASR model, statistically tied with a leading audio LM, but the audio LM was significantly better on every switch-localised metric. Yoruba token error was about 0.97 for nearly all faithful systems. Several audio LMs failed as exact transcribers: they translated, added verbosity or leaked the prompt, depending heavily on the prompt, [arXiv 2609.11786](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.11786)
- (snippet) YECS, a 120 h Yoruba–English corpus (NeurIPS 2026): Whisper, MMS and Omnilingual ASR "fail to generalize" zero-shot to natural code-switching, with WER up to 320%, [NeurIPS 2026 listing](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/179704)
- Lelapa AI Vulavula (vendor): English, Afrikaans, isiZulu and Sesotho, with a claim that its models "handle code switching". Vulavula Live offers real-time isiZulu↔English transcription and translation for contact centres with select enterprise partners. No independent benchmarks were found, [Lelapa Vulavula](https://lelapa.ai/vulavula); [Vulavula Live](https://lelapa.ai/vulavula/live)
- Intron Sahara (vendor claims via a non-official mirror of the launch post): trained on proprietary audio from 18,000+ speakers with 300+ accents across 30+ countries. It lists an "Afri-Names" subset "rich in numbers, fractions, measurements". No published scores were found, [mirror of launch post](https://trendytheme.net/devspoint/intron/?p=5)
- Google USM: 2B parameters, 12M hours of training audio, covers African languages such as Shona, Malagasy, Luganda, Luo, Bambara and Xhosa. It beats Whisper on languages where Whisper is under 40% WER. No per-language African WER is published in the sources found, [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/universal-speech-model-usm-state-of-the-art-speech-ai-for-100-languages/)
- Google's 2022 work on 15 African languages: pooling small datasets multilingually and pre-training on unsupervised audio improves quality for many of them, [arXiv 2208.03067](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.03067)
- Earlier code-switching ASR work on South African soap-opera data covered English, isiZulu, isiXhosa, Setswana and Sesotho (2018, pre-scope), [arXiv 1807.10949](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1807.10949). Multilingual self-supervised representations improve low-resource African code-switched ASR, [arXiv 2311.15077](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.15077)
- Gazetteers: Google's low-resource ASR work (2019, pre-scope) added gazetteer place names to the language model because "named entities such as geographical locations have proven problematic", [arXiv 1909.07285](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.07285). In African NLP, English Wikipedia entity lists served as gazetteers for languages without their own Wikipedia (Dholuo, Nigerian Pidgin), [arXiv 2507.00297](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.00297)
- (snippet) Text-only domain adaptation (shallow fusion plus density-ratio rescoring) for low-resource Hausa medical ASR (title only, not read), [arXiv 2611.05744](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.05744)

### Inferences
- Gate LLM correction on disagreement. The 2501.15310 pattern (helps weak recognisers, hurts strong ones, hurts self-correction) argues against letting an LLM rewrite spans where all three recognisers agree. Have it act only on disagreement spans or low-confidence name and number slots. Do not have the same model family both transcribe and correct.
- Lock numbers. LLM correction deleted measurement values and added insertions on drug names. For trader calls, where amounts, quantities and rates matter, number spans that the recognisers agree on should be frozen before any LLM step. Run the Indian-number normaliser after fusion, not inside the LLM prompt.
- Score name and number accuracy separately (entity recall or PIER over entity tokens). Low WER can hide high entity error, which is exactly the clinical-NE finding.
- Errors cluster at switch points and on the lower-resource side. For Hinglish trader speech, check which recogniser does better on English insertions versus Hindi spans, and weight fusion votes by span language instead of using one global weight.
- Audio-LM transcribers (gpt-transcribe is in that family) can translate or paraphrase depending on the prompt. Add an output-script sanity check: if the OpenAI output is almost all Latin English while Saaras is mostly Devanagari, treat it as a likely translation and down-weight it.
- AfriSwitch's "targeted training beats scale" supports making Saaras the primary voter for Hindi-dominant spans, with gpt-transcribe as a decorrelated second opinion.

### Gaps
- Masakhane speech work, Lacuna Fund or Common Voice Swahili pipelines, and Karya-in-Africa collection: none found in this pass, so there are no concrete pipeline numbers.
- No source on how Vulavula or Sahara handle personal names or numbers (ITN) at the pipeline level beyond marketing copy.
- No per-language African WER for USM.

## 3. Southeast Asian: Vietnamese tone/diacritics, Thai segmentation (CER vs WER), Indonesian/Malay–English, Filipino Taglish: scoring and post-processing practice

### Takeaway
In SE Asia the main pipeline lesson is about scoring. Where word boundaries are not given (Thai) or are syllabic (Vietnamese), WER depends on the segmenter: the same Thai model differs by about 5 WER points between NewMM and DeepCut tokenisation. CER is the usual workaround, and a NAACL 2025 paper argues it tracks human judgement better even for English. Malay–English code-switching gains came from text-only adaptation and synthetic phrase-mixing, not post-processing. No usable Taglish or Vietnamese tone-normalisation sources were found.

### Cited Findings
- Thai wav2vec2: WER of the same model changes with the reference tokeniser, e.g. 16.3% with NewMM versus 11.4% with DeepCut. CER is reported separately, [arXiv 2208.04799](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.04799). The AIResearch Thai eval script exposes the tokeniser choice (newmm/deepcut/syllable) and computes CER after removing spaces, so numbers are only comparable with the tokeniser held fixed, [eval.py](https://huggingface.co/airesearch/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53-th/blame/main/eval.py)
- A SE Asian cloud-ASR benchmark notes that different segmentation methods give different WERs, [arXiv 2210.03580](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.03580)
- "Advocating CER for Multilingual ASR Evaluation" (NAACL Findings 2025): CER correlates more closely with human judgement than WER, even for English. Thai and Vietnamese lack obvious orthographic word separators, so WER can score two acceptable transcriptions as very different, [arXiv 2410.07400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.07400)
- A Thai TTS paper notes that CER avoids the segmentation dependency but "can obscure critical errors, such as mispronounced names or code-switched expressions", [arXiv 2609.03502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.03502)
- Vietnamese: the syllable is the natural lexical unit for ASR. A VLSP 2021 system reports 6.72% syllable error rate, and word-segmenting the labels did not help, [VNU JCSCE](https://jcsce.vnu.edu.vn/index.php/jcsce/article/view/325/136); [vjs.ac.vn 2018](https://vjs.ac.vn/index.php/jcc/article/download/13181/382802/389019). Vietnamese–English code-switching (TSPC, phoneme-centric two-stage) reaches 20.8% WER and says tone-insensitive models confuse similar-sounding items, [arXiv 2509.05983](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05983v1)
- Malay–English: AsyncSwitch adapts Whisper with text-only code-switched data, giving −9.02% relative WER on Malay–English and gains on monolingual Malay and Singlish, [arXiv 2506.14190](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14190v1). An Interspeech 2025 benchmark (BM-EN, ZH-BM, TA-EN) found fine-tuned SeamlessM4T-v2-Large best, and phrase-mixed synthetic code-switched data beat purely multilingual training, [ISCA 2025](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/nguyen25_interspeech.html); [arXiv 2506.14177](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14177v1)
- A Malaysian case study found lexical accuracy metrics may not show whether the *meaning* of Malay–English code-switched conversation survives ASR, [IJACSA paper](https://thesai.org/Downloads/Volume17No10/Paper_21-Beyond_Conventional_ASR_Metrics.pdf)
- Community Malay–English wav2vec2 model card (not peer-reviewed): about 50 h of fine-tuning took WER from 34.29% to 17.26%, [HF model card](https://huggingface.co/langminer/wav2vec2-custom-asr/blob/main/README.md)
- Indonesian–English: semi-supervised machine speech chain on unlabelled data, motivated by scarce paired code-switched data, [SIGUL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.sigul-1.18.pdf)
- CS-FLEURS: Whisper does much worse on code-switched pairs with distinct scripts than on same-script pairs, [arXiv 2509.14161](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14161)

### Inferences
- Hinglish has the same scoring trap as Thai segmentation: the result depends on a pre-processing choice, here *script* rather than segmenter. Parakh's evaluation should fix the normaliser and transliterator and report WER, CER and a script-normalised WER together.
- The Thai TTS caveat (CER hides name errors) means CER alone is the wrong headline for Parakh, whose value is in names and numbers. Report an entity-level metric next to CER.
- CS-FLEURS's distinct-script penalty fits Hinglish written in Devanagari plus Latin. Requesting a single-script view (Sarvam `translit`) for matching and voting removes one source of disagreement between recognisers.

### Gaps
- No Taglish (Tagalog–English) ASR scoring or post-processing source was found.
- No study directly measuring tone-diacritic-insensitive WER for Vietnamese. The Yoruba diacritic-insensitive WER in Section 2 is the nearest analogue.
- No production (vendor) practice found for Indonesian or Malay code-switching post-processing.

## 4. Indic production stacks: Sarvam, AI4Bharat, Bhashini, Karya, Gnani, Navana, Reverie, Exotel and call-centre vendors: Hinglish script choice, names, numbers, 8 kHz telephony, per-segment LID, script routing

### Takeaway
Indic vendors handle script choice as an explicit output mode rather than a post-process. Sarvam Saaras v4 exposes `transcribe` / `translate` / `verbatim` / `translit` / `codemix` modes and `keyterms` (up to 50 terms of 64 characters each). Telephony vendors (Gnani) claim large gains from native 8 kHz training and from unified models over LID-cascades at switch points. Those numbers are vendor-reported and internally inconsistent. Academically, script-normalised scoring (SN-WER, toWER, T-WER) and LLM rescoring with a short descriptive prompt (CLEAR) give measurable, reproducible gains on Hindi–English.

### Cited Findings
**Sarvam (vendor docs)**
- Saaras v4 modes, shown with the example "My phone number is 9840950950": `transcribe` gives "मेरा फोन नंबर है 9840950950" (native script, numbers normalised); `translate` gives English; `verbatim` keeps spoken numbers as words ("नौ आठ चार zero नौ पांच zero..."); `translit` romanises everything ("mera phone number hai 9840950950"); `codemix` writes English words in Latin and Indic words in native script ("मेरा phone number है 9840950950") and is recommended for "Hinglish conversations, mixed-language customer support". `keyterms` is v4-only, up to 50 terms × 64 chars, and the examples include names and places. v3 supports `mode`. The page does not cover 8 kHz/telephony or `prompt`, [Sarvam: select output mode](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference-docs/api-guides-tutorials/speech-to-text/how-to/select-output-mode)
- Saaras covers 23 languages (22 Indian + English) with automatic language detection, [Sarvam Saaras model page](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference-docs/getting-started/models/saaras)

**Gnani (vendor research blog, 13 May 2026, all self-reported, no public test set)**
- Unified Conformer-CTC with a concatenated tokenizer (separate SentencePiece per language joined into about 16–20K tokens, with [HI]/[EN]/[UNK] tags). Intermediate per-frame LID heads, a language-alignment loss and non-peaky CTC. Script handling uses a three-way vocabulary: Devanagari Hindi, Latin English, and a learned vocabulary of frequent *romanised Hindi* subwords, "because a Devanagari/Latin tokenizer alone would misattribute romanized Hindi as English", [Gnani: code-switching at 8 kHz](https://gnani.ai/resources/research/code-switching-at-8khz)
- Table 1 (Hinglish, 8 kHz G.711, SNR −2 to 15 dB; 4,200 utterances each with at least one intra-sentential switch; switch-point MER over a 1-second window around each switch). Monolingual-Hindi 48.7% switch-MER; LID cascade (sliding-window LID 200–500 ms then monolingual ASR) 29.3% switch / 22.8% overall at about 420 ms P95; Whisper-large-v3 31.2 / 25.6; unified 21.4 / 18.2; plus alignment loss 19.8 / 17.1; Gnani 17.9 / 16.2. Inconsistency: a figure on the same page gives 48.7% for the cascade, not monolingual-only. Other claims: native 8 kHz training (no upsampling), codec augmentation with extra noise at switch frames, gains plateau below about 200 h of code-switched telephony data, a code-switched LM by shallow fusion, 14M hours of telephonic audio, [same](https://gnani.ai/resources/research/code-switching-at-8khz)
- Gnani markets Prisma v2.5/v3.0 as trained on native 8 kHz call-centre audio with 16 kHz for app audio, citing 20.3% WER on Indic 8 kHz telephony against 35.5% for an unnamed benchmark (vendor), [Gnani STT API](https://gnani.ai/dev/speech-to-text-api); [news](https://letsdatascience.com/news/gnani-ai-launches-prisma-v30-speech-to-text-model-for-indian-7163adda)

**Exotel (vendor blog; targets, not measurements)**
- Puts language detection *before* ASR as the first pipeline stage. Names romanised spelling variants and mixed Devanagari/Roman input as challenges, mitigated by "normalising code-mixed input to a standard format". Recommends slot-filling accuracy above 95% for amounts, dates and account numbers, and annotation of IFSC codes and UPI handles. WER targets: Hindi under 15%, English under 10%. Says these benchmarks are based on "general conversational AI best practices", not measured results, [Exotel: Hinglish benchmarks for Indian banks](https://exotel.com/blog/hinglish-conversational-ai-benchmarks-for-indian-banks)
- Lists 8 kHz audio, codecs, packet loss and crosstalk as call-centre ASR degraders, [Exotel: best STT APIs](https://exotel.com/blog/best-speech-to-text-apis-real-time-voice-ai/)

**Bhashini / AI4Bharat / Karya**
- Bhashini groups ASR → translation → TTS into one pipeline request under a pipeline ID, and clients first look up supported language pairs per task (third-party client code and guides, not official docs), [HF client code](https://huggingface.co/spaces/pvanand/general_chat/raw/c7ec9348471a4b76e2832ad67dd8b5e80b85c6a2/ai4b.py); [developer guide](https://dilrajs616.github.io/khalis/week1.html). CIIL's Anuvadika, built on Bhashini models, runs a separate LID engine (lid218e) before translating and uses a hybrid transliteration engine, [Anuvadika about](https://anuvadika.ciil.org/en/about)
- IIT Madras MADASR 2.0 system: a multilingual ASR predicts language ID with the transcript, then a second model converts the output to the target native script (script routing as a post-stage), [arXiv 2511.15418](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.15418v1)
- IndicVoices: 7,348 h of read, extempore and conversational audio from 16,237 speakers in 22 languages, with a staged pipeline (preparation, on-field collection, QC, transcription). It produced IndicASR covering all 22 scheduled languages, [arXiv 2403.01926](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.01926). Karya (Microsoft's open-source Android crowdsourcing app with offline sync) was used to collect Kathbath read speech and Svarah accented English, [IndicVoices](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.01926v1); [Svarah, arXiv 2305.15760](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.15760)

**Scoring and correction for Hinglish / Indic**
- SN-WER (MeLLM 2026): converts reference and hypothesis to a language-specific canonical script before WER. No training needed. Narrows inflated model gaps by up to 12% on FLEURS, with smaller or inconsistent effects on noisy Common Voice and token collisions under 0.1%. Recommended as a companion to WER and CER, not a replacement, because the user-facing script is part of quality, [arXiv 2606.02548](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.02548); [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.mellm-1.24/)
- The Whisper-style English normaliser breaks Indic scripts (it strips combining marks) and inflates reported performance, [Manohar & Pillai, arXiv 2409.02449](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02449v4)
- toWER (Google, SLT 2018, pre-scope): map all text to one writing system before scoring, applied to Indic voice-search traffic, [Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/transliteration-based-approaches-to-improve-code-switched-speech-recognition-performance/). Unsupervised, language-agnostic WER standardisation (learning spelling-variant pairs, including transliteration variants of code-mixed words) gave an average 13.28% WER reduction across 35K utterances in 4 languages, and human raters agreed with the normalised pairs, [arXiv 2303.05046](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05046)
- CLEAR (IIIT-Delhi, ICNLSP 2025), on MUCS 2021 Hindi–English (16 kHz tutorials; blind set 95.55% code-switched). Zero-shot Whisper-small: 266.5% S-WER from repetitive output. Decoder-only fine-tune with frozen encoder: 31.12. Adding a short descriptive prompt (e.g. "This transcript is a code-switched text. Mix of devnagri and english words are present. Text is related to tutorials on academic or technical subjects.") cut S-WER by a further 9.73%. LLM rescoring of a beam of 5 (GPT-2 sum of log-probs) reached S-WER 26.9 and T-WER 25.04. GPT-4 used as a −10..10 hypothesis rater reached T-WER 24.82 but cost about $3 per 100 hypotheses. Beams over 5 hurt every scorer. Long or per-utterance dynamic prompts (e.g. a "hi hi en hi" pattern) "mostly fail", and Whisper truncates prompts at 224 tokens. T-WER replaces Devanagari-transliterated English words with English (डिस्क्रिप्शन → description) before scoring, [CLEAR, ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.icnlsp-1.33.pdf)
- (snippet) For Hindi post-ASR correction, smaller fine-tuned models consistently beat larger instruction-tuned LLMs, [Kumar, Krishna, Ramakrishnan, Jyothi, EACL 2026](https://preview.aclanthology.org/ingest-eacl/2026.eacl-short.45.pdf)
- (snippet) CodeVaani uses a code-aware refinement prompt to fix phonetically distorted technical terms in Hindi–English spoken queries ("ask key" → "ASCII"), [arXiv 2511.20654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.20654)

### Inferences
- Use Sarvam modes as free extra "views" for fusion and matching. `codemix` is the natural display format for Hinglish trader calls. `translit` gives a single-script string, useful as the alignment and voting key against gpt-transcribe's (often Latin) output and for sound-based name retrieval. `verbatim` keeps digits as spoken words, which helps check normalised amounts (e.g. confirming "saade teen" → 3.5 or "dedh sau" → 150 against the spoken form). The modes come from one model, so their errors are correlated; use them for normalisation and checks, not as independent votes.
- Spend keyterms carefully. With 50 × 64-char slots on Saaras v4, feed the top-ranked candidate names from per-user memory and discovered context, plus a few domain terms (commodity, unit and payment words). CLEAR shows short descriptive context helps while long or dynamic prompts fail, so keep any OpenAI prompt to a short fixed description plus a few names.
- Do not split audio by language. Even the vendor that sells cascades' alternative shows the LID-cascade is worse at switch points and slower. For a black-box harness the analogue is to fuse whole-call outputs rather than build an LID-split-then-route stage.
- 8 kHz: vendors stress native 8 kHz handling, and neither the Sarvam nor the OpenAI docs found say how they treat 8 kHz input. A quick A/B (native 8 kHz versus 16 kHz resampled, per API) is an hours-scale check.
- Report T-WER or SN-WER next to WER in the demo. Otherwise a correct "phone" written as "फोन" (or the reverse) counts as an error, and fusion may look worse than it is.

### Gaps
- No public technical details found for Navana, Reverie, or AI4Bharat IndicConformer on telephony or Hinglish script choice.
- No official Bhashini documentation of an explicit LID stage or of where transliteration sits in chained pipelines.
- Sarvam's handling of 8 kHz audio and any `prompt` parameter is not documented on the pages fetched.
- Gnani's numbers cannot be checked (no public test set, and its figure and table disagree).

## 5. Code-switching harnesses in general: per-segment LID, merging two monolingual recognisers, LLM-based code-switch correction, transliteration-normalised evaluation (MER, T-WER, PIER), measured gains

### Takeaway
Across language pairs, the evidence says: (1) avoid hard segment-level LID routing, because LID errors cannot be recovered and cost accuracy; parallel decoding plus rescoring or consensus is safer; (2) aggregate WER hides code-switch and entity failures, so score switch points or points of interest separately (PIER: WER improved 5% while PIER worsened 39% relative); (3) errors concentrate on the embedded-language (usually English) spans and at switch boundaries; (4) LLM correction gains are real but come mostly from fine-tuned or rescoring setups, and should be gated; (5) transliteration-then-normalise scoring matches human judgement best.

### Cited Findings
- Separational (segment-then-route) approach: an LID model splits audio into monolingual segments for monolingual recognisers and stitches the outputs. Performance "becomes highly dependent on correctly predicting the language identity at a segment level", [arXiv 2111.15016](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.15016). LID errors in that design "cannot be recovered" (summarised in a survey), [Sitaram et al. survey, arXiv 1904.00784](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.00784)
- Alternative: run two monolingual recognisers (e.g. English and Malay) *in parallel* with no LID step, then rescore the combined hypotheses to produce the code-switched output, [Sitaram et al. survey](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.00784). Modern approach: one model with a concatenated tokenizer doing ASR and LID together, [Dhawan et al., arXiv 2306.08753](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.08753)
- PIER = errors at points of interest / number of points of interest, where points of interest are tagged reference positions (embedded-language words in the paper; tagging is automatic for distinct-script pairs). Fisher ES–EN, Whisper-large: WER 29.43 → 27.94 while PIER 36.41 → 50.78 (+39.47% relative worse). SEAME: MER 53.81 → 52.90 while PIER 64.51 → 68.49. Open-source code: `enesyugan/PIER-CodeSwitching-Evaluation`, [PIER, arXiv 2501.09512](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.09512v2)
- Bayesian factorised adaptation with a small amount of synthetic code-switched data cut errors on code-switched words by 32.87% and overall WER by 5.31% (Interspeech 2026), [Ugan & Waibel, arXiv 2606.21990](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21990)
- ServiceNow (June 2026, synthetic TTS audio; ES–EN, FR–EN, CA-FR–EN, DE–EN; 7 systems). Errors "concentrate on the English portions of utterances rather than the matrix-language portions". Whisper "defaults to translating into English rather than transcribing" when no language is set on code-switched audio. Switch count predicts *whether* an error occurs and Code-Mixing Index predicts its *size*. Deepgram Nova-3 was mid-tier on semantic WER but last or second-to-last on answer error rate (comprehension questions answered from the transcript), so rankings shift between WER and task metrics. The authors suggest forced language tokens or hints might help but did not test them, [HF blog: Can voice agents handle bilingual customers?](https://huggingface.co/blog/ServiceNow-AI/code-switching)
- CoSE-E (enterprise code-switching benchmark, 5 pairs): enterprise speakers stay in their matrix language and switch to English for domain terms ("VPN", "payroll cycle"). It evaluates downstream task outcomes, not just transcripts, because ASR errors propagate, [arXiv 2609.35645](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.35645)
- Generative error correction for code-switching: N-best lists from *multiple* ASR systems are fed to an LLM with a LoRA adapter that learns the hypotheses-to-transcription mapping. It "significantly" reduces MER and is data-efficient (abstract only; Mandarin–English), [Chen et al., arXiv 2310.13013](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13013). LLMs have also been used as plug-and-play hypothesis filters for code-switching without training, [arXiv 2407.04219](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.04219)
- West Frisian (2026 preprint): LLM GER "improves ASR performance in most settings", with the best GPT-5.1 results beating oracle WERs. Gains held on a non-public offline text set built to control for training-data contamination, [arXiv 2605.19711](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19711)
- (snippet) LLMs hallucinate when resolving unknown or rare named entities. Giving the LLM phonetically similar candidate entities from local context helps, [arXiv 2506.10779](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10779v1); [Error-aware TF-IDF RAG for ASR correction, arXiv 2606.24915](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.24915)
- Metrics: on dialectal Arabic–English, "transliteration followed by text normalization" correlated best with human judgement, [arXiv 2211.16319](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.16319). Perle: WER overstates gaps about 3× against BERTScore under transliteration variance, [arXiv 2605.19069](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19069). Unsupervised spelling-variant standardisation: −13.28% average WER, [arXiv 2303.05046](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05046)
- (snippet) A reference-free "script collapse" metric and a 100-pair benchmark for multilingual ASR (title only, not read), [arXiv 2604.08786](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.08786)

### Inferences
**Cross-cutting transfer list for Parakh, ranked by value per hour (inference, built from the findings above):**
1. **Normalise before you vote.** Build a matching key (transliterate to one script with Sarvam `translit` or a transliteration library, lowercase, apply the Devanagari nukta/anusvara/ZWJ folding from Section 1, cluster romanised spelling variants as in 2303.05046) and run alignment and voting (ROVER-style) on that key. Otherwise "rupaye/rupees/रुपये" split their votes. Display text still comes from the winning `codemix` string.
2. **Entity-PIER as the headline metric.** Tag name and number tokens in references as points of interest and report PIER over them, plus WER and T-WER or SN-WER. PIER only needs tagged positions, so this applies directly (the paper itself does not test names).
3. **Cross-recogniser prompting.** Saaras output → shuffled keyword prompt plus top names → gpt-transcribe second pass (Talafha). Keep prompts short (CLEAR's 224-token limit; dynamic prompts failed). Watch for prompt continuation.
4. **Consensus selection (proxy-guided):** choose the candidate with minimum average distance to the other recognisers' outputs, at whole-utterance or span level.
5. **Gated LLM correction:** only on disagreement spans or name/number slots, with sound-alike candidate lists in the prompt (DeRAGEC-style, already covered). Freeze agreed numbers. Never let a model correct its own output. In the clinical study, correction slightly hurt the strongest ASR.
6. **Span-language-aware weighting:** errors concentrate on English insertions and at switch points. Measure each recogniser's accuracy on English versus Hindi spans and weight votes accordingly.
7. **Translation and script-collapse guard:** always pass language and mode explicitly to every recogniser (Whisper translates when it is unset), and flag outputs whose script ratio diverges sharply from the others.
8. **Task-level evaluation:** for trader calls, score extraction outcomes (who, how much, what item, what rate) as CoSE-E and ServiceNow do, since WER rankings and task rankings can differ.

### Gaps
- No published numbers found for black-box-API-only fusion (no n-best, no logits) on Hinglish. All GER results use n-best or fine-tuned adapters.
- MER definitions vary between papers (Mandarin-character/English-word mixed error rate versus Gnani's "edit distance without penalising script mismatch"). No single standard.
- Full numeric results of 2310.13013 (GER for code-switching) were not retrieved; only the abstract was read.

## 6. European minority languages (Welsh, Basque, Catalan, Irish): public-body production practice: user-correction loops, vocabulary lists, name gazetteers

### Takeaway
The public bodies' strongest practical lessons are about product loops rather than models. Bangor's Welsh Trawsgrifiwr uses VAD segmentation and a per-segment listen/correct/commit interface with explicit opt-in before corrected data is reused. Irish Fotheidil folds human corrections back into training. Welsh sentence collection uses lexicon allow-lists (dictionary plus a 20,000-word permitted list that deliberately keeps commonly used English brand and company names). Constraining the vocabulary to a domain is the single biggest measured gain: Welsh voice-assistant WER 4.18% with a domain template-plus-slot LM against 18.06% with a general LM.

### Cited Findings
**Welsh (Bangor University Language Technologies Unit, CLTW 2022)**
- Trawsgrifiwr Ar-lein: a submitted file or YouTube link is segmented with webrtcvad and each segment transcribed. The user gets a unique URL with per-segment playback, the automatic transcript and a text box. A tick/disk button commits each correction, next and previous segments are shown for context, short segments can be merged, and output exports to SRT or Praat TextGrid. Content is automatically deleted after 30 days and not reused unless users *opt in* to donate audio and corrected transcripts to the open test corpus, [Jones 2022, CLTW](https://aclanthology.org/2022.cltw-1.8.pdf)
- Vocabulary allow-list for collecting Common Voice sentences: under 15 words, no numbers, acronyms or abbreviations, and every word either in the Welsh lexicon or in a list of 20,000 extra permitted words. This filtered out many American English proper nouns, while "certain English words, as well as company names and products … commonly used in Welsh speech" were kept via the permitted list. 130,502 sentences were excluded, [same](https://aclanthology.org/2022.cltw-1.8.pdf)
- Domain-constrained LM: the Macsen voice assistant's LM was generated from template sentences with slot-value entity lists (e.g. "names of Welsh language bands"), 1,098 sentences in total. Coqui STT with this domain LM scored 4.18% WER / 2.4% CER against 18.06% / 5.11% for the stronger general wav2vec2-xls-r-1b plus a general 5-gram LM, [same](https://aclanthology.org/2022.cltw-1.8.pdf)
- A general 5-gram KenLM (OSCAR, 23M words) cut wav2vec2-xls-r-1b WER from 22.14 to 13.33 (−39.79%) on Common Voice. On real transcription-app audio the best was 32.96 WER, with free-spoken, code-switched videos much worse (up to 102.8 WER). English spans in references were tagged [en_start]/[en_finish], [same](https://aclanthology.org/2022.cltw-1.8.pdf)

**Irish (ABAIR / Trinity College Dublin)**
- Fotheidil: a web transcription system combining off-the-shelf VAD and diarisation with Irish-specific ASR and capitalisation/punctuation restoration. Users edit text, timestamps and speaker labels. "Human-corrected transcriptions will be collected and included in the training dataset as the system is used", described as a cyclical, community-driven loop with no retraining cadence given. Semi-supervised learning on unlabelled radio cut WER on hand-labelled radio from 22.1 to 16.1 (about 27% relative), and the least-represented dialect (Ulster) gained most. A single seq2seq model restoring capitalisation, punctuation, digits and acronyms together beat a BERT classifier (WERpc 8.40 vs 9.38). Irish initial mutations break naive capitalisation ("i nGaeilge", "ón bhFrainc"), [Fotheidil, arXiv 2501.00509](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00509v1)
- A "Global" pronunciation lexicon using dialect-invariant phonemes where dialects diverge beat a multi-dialect lexicon across Ulster, Connacht and Munster (best overall WER 8.74%), [Lonergan et al., Interspeech 2022](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2022/lonergan22_interspeech.pdf)

**Basque (HiTZ / Aholab, University of the Basque Country)**
- Albayzin 2024 bilingual Basque–Spanish challenge: a Conformer-Transducer trained on about 1,622 h of Spanish, Basque and code-switched speech reached 2.67% WER on test (3.02% on dev, Basque Parliament data) using mAES decoding with LM rescoring, [HiTZ-AhoLab, IberSPEECH 2024](https://www.isca-archive.org/iberspeech_2024/herranz24_iberspeech.html)
- An earlier fully bilingual system *avoids language segmentation*: one acoustic unit set and one aggregated LM, trained semi-supervised on Basque Parliament data, [Penagarikano et al., IberSPEECH 2022](https://www.isca-archive.org/iberspeech_2022/penagarikano22_iberspeech.html). (snippet) Another project fine-tunes Whisper for Basque, keeps the base model for Spanish, and routes segments after language labelling, [UC3M Cátedra](https://catedramasmovil.uc3m.es/en/?p=1931). EuskañolDS is a naturally sourced Basque–Spanish code-switching corpus, [arXiv 2502.03188](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.03188v1)

**Catalan (Projecte AINA / BSC / Generalitat)**
- AINA launched in 2022 (Generalitat + BSC, €3M) and collected voices through Mozilla Common Voice (over 25,500 Catalan contributors by 2022). Central Catalan was over 75% of recordings, a known dialect gap, so AINA ran a tour of Catalan-speaking territories to add dialect voices, [BSC news](https://bsc.es/news/bsc-news/aina-project-goes-tour-through-catalan-speaking-territories-add-voices-all-dialectal-variants); [BSC](https://bsc.es/node/58405)
- AINA's Whisper-large-v3 Catalan fine-tune (710 h, "3CatParla"): 10.32 WER on CV17. It outputs lowercase text without punctuation, leaving formatting to downstream stages, [HF model card](https://huggingface.co/projecte-aina/whisper-large-v3-ca-3catparla). Catalan Common Voice Scripted Speech 25.0 was released March 2026 under CC0, [Mozilla Data Collective](https://datacollective.mozillafoundation.org/datasets/cmnd4la5a02fwmh074t1fx5y9)

**Frisian (European minority, LLM correction)**
- GPT-5.1 GER improved low-resource Frisian ASR in most settings, checked on a non-public offline set to rule out contamination, [arXiv 2605.19711](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19711)

### Inferences
- Copy the Trawsgrifiwr interface for Parakh's learning loop: per-segment playback, one-tap confirm or edit, and only then write the learned name to per-user memory. Add an explicit opt-in before corrections are used beyond that user, and auto-delete audio after N days. This fits the "remembers learned names per user" feature and gives a privacy story for judges.
- The Welsh allow-list keeps loanwords and brand names people actually say. For Parakh, the per-user plus discovered name list should likewise include English brand, product and unit names that traders use (inference from CoSE-E's English-for-domain-terms finding).
- The template-plus-slot domain LM gain (4.18 vs 18.06 WER) is the closed-vocabulary version of Parakh's retrieval. When the set of plausible names is small and known (a trader's contacts and suppliers), constraining or verifying against it is worth far more than general modelling. Black-box analogue: keyterms plus post-hoc snapping of near-miss names to the list with a threshold, as in Tarteel's 85% fuzzy-match rule, sending sub-threshold cases to user confirmation.
- Fotheidil's joint capitalisation/punctuation/digits model suggests doing Indian-number ITN and formatting in one well-tested deterministic pass after fusion, rather than spreading formatting across recognisers that each format differently (inference).
- The Basque experience (segmentation-free bilingual model beats language-routed pipelines; segment-routing variants exist but are fragile) matches the Arabic and Gnani evidence against hard LID routing.

### Gaps
- No public documentation found of a *name gazetteer* in Welsh, Irish, Basque or Catalan production ASR. The closest are the Welsh lexicon allow-list and the Macsen slot lists.
- No information found on Orai (Basque) speech work, Aholab production deployments, or whether AINA models run in Catalan public services.
- No measured effect of the Trawsgrifiwr or Fotheidil correction loops on later model accuracy (both describe the loop, not its gains).
